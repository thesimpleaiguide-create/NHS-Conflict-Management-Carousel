MDE behaviours do not always appear in isolation. A single interaction can move through all three.

Phase 1

Entitlement

"I have been coming here for twenty years..."

Leverages personal status or clinic longevity to demand exceptions to normal protocols.

Phase 2

Demanding Behaviour

"I need to be seen today, I do not care how full you are..."

Insists on immediate compliance, overriding capacity constraints or clinical triage criteria.

Phase 3

Manipulation

"I will be making a formal complaint and I know exactly who to speak to..."

Applies emotional leverage, threats of escalation, or name dropping to force compliance.

Key Strategic Takeaway

The shift is rarely random. When one approach stops working, the patient moves to another. Each escalation is an attempt to find the tactic that will finally get them what they need.

Understanding that makes the pattern predictable. And predictable is manageable.

When MDE Behaviours Escalate

MDE behaviours tend to intensify when they meet resistance. What begins as a firm request can become a demand. A demand can become a threat.

1
Starting Point

Firm Request → Entitlement

"I expect to be seen by my preferred doctor because of my longstanding history here."
2
Secondary Escalation

Demand → Pressure Tactics

"I don't care if the clinic is full. I am not leaving until I am given an appointment today."
3
Final Pivot

Threat → Coercive Manipulation

"I will report you personally to management and take this to the local committee."

The Mindset Shift: Once you recognise the pattern, you are no longer caught off guard by it. You can stay calm, neutral, and consistent in your response.

Differentiating MDE from Genuine Distress

Not every frustrated or difficult patient is displaying MDE behaviour.

Situational Distress / Frustration

  • • A patient who is short-tempered after a long wait in reception.
  • • A patient who becomes emotional or tearful when discussing a diagnosis.
  • • Anxious patients struggling to navigate online access or phone systems.

Persistent MDE Behaviour

  • • Describes specific, persistent patterns of behaviour.
  • • Continues even after reasonable explanations and alternatives have been offered.
  • • Calculated shifts between status leverage, pressure, and threats.

Why it matters: The distinction matters because the response is different. Distress requires empathy and clarity; MDE requires firm, calm boundaries and consistent policy enforcement.

Worked Example: Mrs Patel Scenario

Mrs Patel arrives at the desk without an appointment. She is well-dressed, confident and speaks with authority.

1. Entitlement First 00:00 - Arrival
"I need to see Dr Andrews today. I have been a patient here for fifteen years and I always see him."
Clinic Action: Reception polite and firm: explains Dr Andrews is fully booked for today.
2. Demanding Behaviour Second 00:45 - Tone Shift
"This is completely unacceptable. I need to be seen today. I do not care about your system."
Clinic Action: Reception holds boundary: offers an alternative appointment with a different GP.
3. Manipulation Third 01:30 - Final Pivot
"I think you should know that my husband is on the Patient Participation Group. I would hate for this to become something more formal."
Clinic Action: Mrs Patel leans forward, attempting to induce fear of formal reprimand.
Scenario Breakdown

Within two minutes, all three MDE behaviours have appeared. Entitlement first. Demanding behaviour second. Manipulation third.

Each one triggered by the failure of the last.
